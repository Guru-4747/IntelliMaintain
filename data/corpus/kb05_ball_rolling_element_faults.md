---
title: Rolling Element (Ball) Faults - Signatures, Causes and Inspection Actions
type: maintenance guideline (project knowledge base)
---

# 1. Vibration signature of a ball (rolling element) fault

A defect on a rolling element strikes the inner and outer race alternately as the ball spins, so the characteristic frequency is twice the ball spin frequency (2 x BSF), often with sidebands at the cage frequency (FTF) because the defective ball moves in and out of the load zone with the cage. Ball defects frequently produce weaker and less regular signatures than race defects: the ball can change its spin axis, so the defect does not hit the races on every rotation. As a consequence, envelope spectra of ball faults are often inconclusive, and diagnosis relies more on overall impulsiveness indicators (kurtosis, crest factor), high-frequency band energy and data-driven classifiers.

# 2. Typical root causes

Ball defects are caused by contamination (hard particles rolled into the ball surface), inadequate lubrication, material inclusions, electrical erosion, or damage during handling and mounting. A single damaged ball can quickly damage the raceways, so a ball defect should be treated as a precursor to race damage.

# 3. Recommended actions when a ball fault is indicated

1. Because envelope confirmation is often weak for ball faults, confirm the prediction by trending kurtosis, crest factor and high-frequency energy over several measurements rather than relying on a single reading.
2. Check the lubricant for contamination and metal particles; check seals and shields for damage.
3. Increase the measurement frequency and look for the appearance of BPFO or BPFI components, which indicate that the damage has spread to the raceways.
4. Plan bearing replacement at the next convenient stop. A bearing with a rolling-element defect cannot be repaired in the field.
5. After removal, inspect all rolling elements and both raceways and document the failure mode.

# 4. Diagnostic caution

When a classifier predicts a ball fault but no characteristic frequency is visible in the envelope spectrum, the result should be presented as uncertain and confirmed by an experienced analyst. Conversely, when the envelope spectrum shows strong inner race or outer race harmonics, these should be investigated even if the classifier predicts a ball fault.
