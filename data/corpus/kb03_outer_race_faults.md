---
title: Outer Race Faults - Signatures, Causes and Inspection Actions
type: maintenance guideline (project knowledge base)
---

# 1. Vibration signature of an outer race fault

An outer race defect on a stationary outer ring is struck by every rolling element as it passes through the defect location. This produces a series of impacts at the ball pass frequency of the outer race (BPFO). Because the defect does not move relative to the load zone, the impact amplitude is nearly constant and the envelope spectrum shows a clean series of BPFO harmonics (1x, 2x, 3x BPFO) usually without shaft-speed sidebands. Time-domain kurtosis is typically high (often above 5) and crest factor is elevated at the early and middle stages of damage. A defect located in the load zone (6 o'clock position) produces the strongest signature; defects outside the load zone (3 or 12 o'clock) can be much weaker and may be missed by amplitude thresholds.

# 2. Typical root causes

Common root causes of outer race damage are: rolling contact fatigue at the end of the rated life; inadequate or degraded lubrication causing surface distress; hard particle contamination producing indentations that grow into spalls; a housing seat that is out of round or too tight (pinched outer ring), producing two load zones; static overload or impact during handling that creates brinell marks spaced at the ball pitch; and electrical current passage, which produces fluting (washboard pattern) on the outer race.

# 3. Recommended actions when an outer race fault is indicated

When the classifier and envelope spectrum indicate an outer race fault with high kurtosis, the following actions are recommended:

1. Verify the finding: repeat the measurement, confirm the BPFO peak and harmonics in the envelope spectrum and check that the shaft speed used for the calculation is correct.
2. Increase monitoring frequency (for example from monthly to weekly or daily) and trend RMS, kurtosis and the BPFO envelope amplitude.
3. Check lubrication: confirm the correct grease or oil, quantity and relubrication interval; take a lubricant sample if possible and look for metallic particles.
4. Inspect the housing fit and alignment at the next opportunity.
5. Plan bearing replacement at the next scheduled stop. Replace immediately if RMS rises rapidly, temperature increases, audible noise appears or envelope amplitude grows across consecutive measurements.
6. After removal, inspect the outer ring raceway, record the damage location relative to the load zone and classify the failure mode (for example according to ISO 15243) to address the root cause.

# 4. Severity guidance

Small isolated spalls (early stage) often show high kurtosis with only modest RMS increase. As the spall grows, RMS increases and kurtosis may decrease because the impacts merge. A combination of rising RMS, rising broadband noise floor and falling kurtosis indicates advanced damage and a short remaining life. The decision to run to the next stop or to stop immediately must be taken by the responsible maintenance engineer.
