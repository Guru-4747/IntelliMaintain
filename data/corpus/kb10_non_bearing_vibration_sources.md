# 1. Why non-bearing faults matter for bearing diagnosis

Many vibration problems that are reported as "bearing noise" are caused by other machine faults. These faults also shorten bearing life because they increase bearing load. Before replacing a bearing, the analyst should rule out unbalance, misalignment and mechanical looseness.

# 2. Unbalance

Unbalance produces a dominant sinusoidal component at 1x running speed in the radial direction, with low kurtosis (close to 3 or below) and a crest factor close to that of a sine wave. Amplitude increases with the square of speed. Correction is by field balancing; replacing the bearing does not fix unbalance.

# 3. Misalignment

Shaft misalignment between coupled machines produces high 1x and 2x running-speed components, often with high axial vibration and a phase difference of about 180 degrees across the coupling. Misalignment increases bearing loads and can cause premature raceway wear along one edge. Correction is by laser or dial-indicator alignment.

# 4. Mechanical looseness

Looseness of bearing housings, foundation bolts or a bearing that turns on its shaft produces many harmonics of running speed (1x, 2x, 3x ... often up to 10x) and sometimes half-order components. Looseness of a bearing in its housing can also produce random impacting that raises kurtosis, so it can be confused with a bearing defect. Check bolt torque and fits before concluding that the bearing itself is damaged.

# 5. Electrical sources in motors

Electrically induced vibration at twice line frequency (100 Hz on a 50 Hz supply, 120 Hz on a 60 Hz supply) disappears immediately when power is switched off, whereas mechanical vibration decays with speed. Shaft currents from variable frequency drives can cause electrical erosion (fluting) of bearing raceways; insulated bearings or shaft grounding are used as countermeasures.
